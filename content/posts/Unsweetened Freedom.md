title: Unsweetened Freedom
---

Human freedom is undeniable. We may argue about constraints and conditions that inevitably influence and even limit our capacity of choice. These constraints could also be an active, consciously-made choice by the agent (decision maker) in order to achieve something. It could also be some external force outside of their control. Yet, freedom is still there, even if consequences seem likely to happen, the choice is still ours.

Trying to deny one's freedom is to say that we do not have control of our actions. In other words, that we are nothing but the mannequins of an unpredictable environment with no means of actions, just reactions. Animals are not self-aware the same way we are. Unlike other animals, humans are specially burdened by their own reflectiveness: we do not only act, but we also know that we are acting. The animal doesn't attain self-awareness even if its safety is clear and needs are covered. In the same way, a human doesn't suddenly lose its freedom when trapped behind bars or pressed by the responsibility of school or family. It can surely be constrained but freedom is still present. You are as free as an inmate is in jail.

This concept can be misunderstood very easily, because, *How can an inmate be as free as me?*
The idea of freedom doesn't limit itself to the amount of possibilities that we have available, freedom is the capacity of choice. If this wasn't true, there would be no need for guards to exist in prison. Inmates are still free, but an external agent is limiting the choices that they have available. Their freedom is limited but not completely revoked. They can still choose to get into a brawl during recreation hours, as well as they can choose to work out during bedtime. The same applies to those outside the cage.

You may wake up every day, go to work, come back, sleep, and repeat. How is this different from the routine an inmate has? The only difference is that we have a lot more options to choose from each day, although we already have boxed ourselves into a small array of possibilities just to eliminate the dread that freedom provokes upon us.

Through choices, people create the constraints necessary for them to block their perceived freedom, almost trying to deny or reduce it to: *"I'm not completely free!"* , where freedom is denied in some instances but celebrated in others. This *simulated ignorance* is what keeps us in the comfort zone where life becomes a big set of steps or *hypothetical imperatives* [^1] where we do not care about finding value in the recognition of our capacity to choose. I do not blame them, it is natural for everyone to avoid looking into the abyss; a place with endless possibilities where you realize that any option is an option.  The product of this awareness is *anxiety*.

Anxiety happens when we look into an interminable number of options and realize that all of them are available. It is necessary for our existence to accept some options as predetermined---default responses to *common* situations, things like the process of washing dishes, how to drive a car safely, eating without biting your tongue off. All of these may seem as dumb examples, but they actually are the ones that propose the greatest anxiety.  When washing dishes, through experience you already decided that you grab a knife from the handle, or to not squeeze a cup of glass too much. Still, you are free to stab yourself or to smash the cup onto your head, that is still an option. Could you imagine having to decide how to do these things every single time you do them? Although we are free, we still must rely on certain decisions in order to avoid the dread. It is easy to imagine how stressful life would be to eat while actively trying to avoid eating your own tongue.

You may not experience anxiety in these cases because of the mechanisms that we evolved with. These *practical habits* are the things that we learn through experience to do. It allows us to avoid wasting unnecessary resources thinking about minor details of existence. These are a type of *shade* that we create in order to have a more pleasant life. The problem  lies in the fact that the anxiety that exists from mere *habitual* existence is---in the big scheme of things---the same type of dread that we face about other decisions.

It becomes a habit to wake up every day, commute to work, come back, sleep, and repeat. Just like stabbing yourself is still an option, skipping work is also an option. People hide themselves behind structure, they may value what they do every day, but most of the time they do not stop to question what they actually value. They might think that their six-figure job is valuable, but they think so just because is what society believes to be precious.[^2] This *existential avoidance* is a cowardly way of living where the agent fails to recognize their ability to freely choose just to run away from the dread of facing freedom.

Practical habits protect the mind from unnecessary decision-making. Existential avoidance occurs when a person uses structure not to merely function, but to avoid the responsibility and weight of their choices. If such structure is removed, we are left facing freedom directly. If we are to remove all those things that provide a sense of stability, we are left with the raw shape of our free life. Although structure essentially *hides* it, freedom is always present but masked. It is the removal of that mask and our inability to act upon it that brings a profound feeling of dread and despair.

This is the reason why breaks can become dark times for many. The level of dread that one may experience upon this tension is directly related to how aware the individual is of its own freedom. You definitely can surrender to a level of existence where you do whatever others suggest for you, but this would not help you feel fulfilled, just entertained. 

Although structure itself is not inherently bad, the simulated ignorance of one's life can become a point of crisis. Moreover, learning to live without a structure is a challenge that must be faced. Even if the individual is aware of his structure and find fulfillment in it, he still needs to find happiness outside of it. Breaks reveal the burden of self-direction by pushing a person to do the things that he likes but using only his will and desire, knowing that if he does or does not do so nothing will happen. There are no societal consequences to his actions, but there is a personal disappointment with oneself. This quieter and forceful punishment comes from the inside. The inner judge knows the values the individual claims to hold, and it punishes him when those values remain unchosen.

The dread of breaks is not just mere boredom, laziness, or lack of entertainment. It is the exposure to *unsweetened* freedom: the individual realizes that he is responsible for pursuing his interests. There are no external demands for his participation in life and the world remains indifferent to his actions, yet the individual remains responsible for pursuing what he claims to value. The individual becomes, at the same time, judge, jury, and executioner.





[^1]: Immanuel Kant explains the Hypothetical imperative as a set of steps that do not apply at all times in all cases but rather an active choice dependent of the circumstances; "If I want X, then I must do Y." In other words, if you will an end, you must will the means necessary to achieve it.

[^2]: With this, I'm not saying that a job is not really important or valuable. Is more a question of society's-induced value into personal life vs real personal value. You may value your six-figure job because you find it purposeful or meaningful, the same way you could  "value" it just because it is a *six-figure job*.
